## Key Commercial Insights

### 1. Founders Law operates a flexible "pod" model rather than fixed retainer/billable-hour law firm structure

Clients get a pod (akin to an account management team) as their day-to-day contact, which can be as embedded as the client wants — plugged into Slack/Teams/email and switched on or off, which keeps costs down for earlier-stage clients without in-house counsel. Specialists (fundraising, employment, data privacy, IP, sector specialists in fintech, health tech, crypto, AI) are pulled in as needed rather than the client being bounced between departments and repeatedly re-briefing new people.

### 2. Their focus has moved upmarket to Seed/Series A/B, with early-stage clients treated as pipeline rather than core business

Tilly was explicit: early-stage founders "don't have the money to pay for our services" and rarely generate repeat work, so while Founders Law still works with them (partly out of genuine affinity), they are not the commercial focus. This mirrors Brom's own realisation about Lean Otter's target market.

### 3. As clients scale further (Series C/D+), Founders Law wraps around in-house counsel rather than competing with big-name firms

Once a company reaches the point where in-house hiring makes sense, Founders Law shifts into a support role — covering gaps like maternity leave or holiday cover, or helping a team build the internal case to justify further in-house hires. At the more advanced end (Series C/D, heavier fundraising), clients often still want a big-name firm alongside them (Tilly named Mishcon de Reya and Osborne Clarke as examples), and Founders Law works alongside rather than instead of those firms.

### 4. Due diligence is a major, recurring part of their work, always acting founder-side

Tilly described "lots and lots" of DD work, always for the founder/company side of a raise — Founders Law has relationships with VCs but never acts for them. Preparedness varies widely: some clients prepare diligence materials well in advance of a raise, others scramble at the same time as the raise process starts, and Tilly said they push hard on early preparation because they've seen "horror stories" from being under-prepared.

### 5. Exit/raise readiness is a recognised but still under-marketed need — a direct parallel to Lean Otter's positioning

Founders Law is speaking on an "exit readiness" panel at Women's Health Week in ~October 2026 (roughly a month from the call date), addressing what founders should be doing from Series A onwards to think long-term. Tilly's own read is that the market broadly understands the logic of pre-emptive readiness work but it "doesn't feel like the number one thing they're aware exists" — closely echoing the awareness challenge Brom described for Lean Otter's product-readiness/pre-diligence offer.
