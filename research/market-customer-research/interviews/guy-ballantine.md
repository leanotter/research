## Key Commercial Insights

### 1. PE firms are creatures of habit

Funds run a standard checklist of due diligence workstreams — commercial, financial, legal, tech, and increasingly cyber as part of tech DD — and getting them to add an entirely new category is genuinely difficult. That said, it is not impossible: he pointed to management and psychometric testing as a DD category that barely existed twenty years ago and is now close to standard practice, once a credible advocate proved its value.

### 2. The sell side is the more accessible route to build credibility

Guy was clear that the smartest entry point is not private equity directly, but the large population of founder-led SaaS and tech-enabled businesses that are growing, underinvested, and thinking about an exit. These owners often do not understand their own product, users, or competitive position as well as they think, and there is likely repeat, fee-generating work available there well before a buy-side PE relationship is realistic.

### 3. Buy-side traction depends on being adopted, not winning deals one at a time

Trying to win individual DD engagements deal-by-deal across many different funds is a slow, exhausting way to build a book. The realistic path in is finding one active, receptive fund that tries you on a live deal, likes the output, and then folds you into their standard roster of providers — after which the work becomes comparatively easy to repeat. He cited a warranty and indemnity insurance broker for PE deals that built exactly this kind of specialist niche over several years and now works with around ten funds.

### 4. Win with a sharp, evidence-based problem statement

Rather than pitching "we do product due diligence," the stronger approach is to identify the two or three specific things that generic tech DD consistently misses and lead with that, because most funds already believe product is covered by their existing tech DD provider. A "start with why" framing — why they need this given what they are not currently catching — is more likely to land than a capabilities-led pitch.

### 5. Generic tech DD often only skims the surface of product

He used a live, current example to make the point concrete: Joe Sanchez, CEO of a SaaS business mid-acquisition, is trying to establish whether the target's product has genuine feature-and-function parity with his own — but that work is being done informally, largely by his own chief of staff via a spreadsheet, rather than by a specialist. Standard tech DD had already confirmed the target's platform was technically robust and reliable, but left a large, unresolved gap around whether the actual product substance matched what was being claimed.

## How This Market Buys

- On the sell side, founders and owners typically go to an M&A adviser first, who builds the information memorandum; below roughly £5m of EBITDA, the product and tech narrative in that document is often thin or skipped entirely.
- On the buy side, PE funds run a standard, largely fixed DD checklist and rarely add a new workstream without a specific trigger or internal champion.
- Buy-side work is won through relationships and being in the right place at the right time with an active, deal-doing fund — not through direct, cold outreach to firms.
- Once trusted, a specialist gets folded into a fund's repeat roster and the work becomes close to automatic on future deals.

## Where Our Offer Fits

- Sell-side preparation: shaping the product and tech narrative in the information memorandum, and identifying roadmap or investment-story gaps before a process starts — the most accessible near-term opportunity in Guy's view.
- Positioning as an "insurance policy" against a failed sale process, which is highly damaging and can effectively block a business from market for 12–24 months.
- Buy-side diligence: a real, demonstrated gap exists, but entry is slower and depends on being pulled in by an active, receptive fund.
- Guy's own client base — founders pre-process and PE-backed CEOs post-deal — is a natural, ongoing referral channel in both directions.

## Diligence / Value Creation Priorities

- Standard tech DD tends to confirm technical robustness and reliability (uptime, engineering quality) but frequently stops there.
- Genuine product substance — feature and function parity against competitors or acquisition targets, roadmap credibility, real user sentiment — is often left an unknown, sometimes handled informally by an internal exec rather than a specialist.
- Missed product and tech debt can be severe and expensive to discover late: he cited roughly £2m of undisclosed tech debt found within days of the Thomas acquisition completing, a cost still being felt.
- The evolution of management and psychometric DD from novel to near-standard practice is a useful precedent for how a new category can be established over time.

## Positioning And Messaging Implications

- Avoid a generic "we do product due diligence" pitch — most funds believe this is already covered by their tech DD provider.
- Lead with a specific, evidence-backed problem statement naming the two or three things generic tech DD consistently misses on the product side.
- Use concrete, credible case material to make the problem tangible rather than abstract — the Thomas tech-debt story and the live Joe Sanchez feature-parity gap are both strong examples.
- Frame the pitch around "why" the current process has a blind spot, not just "what" the service does.
