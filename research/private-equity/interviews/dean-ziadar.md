## Snapshot

Dean is a partner at LCV Partners, a firm that builds sector "theses" for private equity clients: identifying fragmented markets, clustering groups of companies within them, and getting those companies acquisition-ready for a PE buyer. He works across four verticals — field service management/industrial maintenance software, vertical software (accountancy, legal, consulting), and defense/drone dual-use hardware, among others — and described a specific roll-up playbook where several struggling-but-customer-rich software companies are bought together, their client bases combined, and one product is chosen and accelerated using AI-native development.

His core thesis was that generic, non-proprietary software is being commoditised by the collapse in the cost of building software with AI, and that proprietary data flowing through a digital product — not the software itself — is now the main driver of deal value and multiple. On product due diligence specifically, he was candid that his own experience presenting detailed product findings to PE clients has largely fallen flat; PE's real focus post-acquisition is revenue synergy and cost-out, not product substance. He was warm and generous with his time, and offered a direct introduction to a partner colleague, Jerry Griffin, who is active in the learning tech / HR space and currently evaluating a live acquisition there.

## Key Commercial Insights

### 1. AI is collapsing the barrier to entry for building software, which is reshaping which products survive

Dean's view is that low-code and AI-assisted development tools mean small teams can now move as fast as much larger ones used to, which is accelerating fragmentation across the software market. Companies that adopt AI-native development will pull ahead; those that stick with traditional methods will fall behind and eventually die — he used SAP as an example of a company already losing share pre-AI that he expects "AI to finish off" within five to ten years.

### 2. LCV's roll-up thesis is built specifically around this dynamic

Rather than buying a single strong software company, LCV's playbook is to acquire a cluster of struggling-but-customer-rich companies in the same space, combine their client bases, pick the single best-fit product among them, and then accelerate that one product's development using AI tooling and AI-fluent talent — effectively using the acquired customer relationships as the asset and the product as the delivery vehicle.

### 3. Proprietary data, not the software itself, is what drives deal value and multiple

The clearest "what changes a deal" signal he gave was proprietary data flowing through a digital product. His example: project management software for wind and solar farm construction was extended to also operate and monitor the farms, meaning operational data started flowing through the tool. In deals like this, buyers negotiate not just for the product but for (often anonymised) access to that data, since aggregating it across a portfolio unlocks predictive capabilities no single farm's data could — and that data access materially improves the achievable multiple. Data that is public or easily replicated, by contrast, is losing value quickly because AI has erased the speed advantage of having built something first.

### 4. Standalone product due diligence has a real but narrow lane on the buy side

Asked directly whether there's an opportunity for deeper buy-side product diligence, Dean's honest answer leaned toward no, for two reasons. Pre-acquisition, PE firms are often not looking for the product to be in great shape — a weaker product is sometimes exactly the opportunity thesis (bring in a stronger platform, cross-sell into the acquired base). Post-acquisition, he said PE sponsors are usually far more focused on cross-sell and reducing operational footprint than on the product itself; he described presenting detailed product reports in the past and being met with polite interest rather than action ("that's interesting... but how do we increase revenue... can we just buy another company?").

### 5. Where he does see room: highly technical, IP- or infrastructure-specific diligence

He qualified his scepticism by noting that if the offer is narrowly targeted — genuinely deep expertise in specific IP or infrastructure questions, rather than generic product review — there is a niche, because otherwise "the tech is the IO and a couple of consultants... sort it out," implying most buy-side teams default to a shallow, generalist tech review unless someone gives them a specific reason not to.

## How This Market Buys

- LCV originates and packages roll-up opportunities for PE clients rather than buying directly — they build the thesis, cluster the targets, and prepare the group for sale.
- Pre-acquisition, PE interest in product quality is often inverted from what a seller would expect: a mediocre product can be a green light (headroom to improve via a stronger platform) rather than a red flag.
- Post-acquisition, PE attention gravitates to commercial synergy (cross-sell, revenue growth, reduced operational cost) far more than to product substance — in Dean's direct experience, detailed product findings have not changed PE decision-making in the deals he's been part of.
- Proprietary, hard-to-replicate data embedded in a digital product is treated as a distinct, separately negotiated asset that can move pricing and deal terms.

## Where Our Offer Fits

- A generalist "we review the product" pitch is the version Dean was sceptical of — his lived experience is that this doesn't land with PE post-acquisition.
- A narrower framing around proprietary data — what data a product generates, how defensible/replicable it is, and what it's worth in a portfolio context — maps closely to what he described as the actual value driver in the deals he's seen, and is a more specific, evidence-based angle than generic product health.
- The learning tech / HR vertical is a live, concrete opportunity: Jerry Griffin is actively evaluating an acquisition there right now and is a direct, warm introduction.
- LCV's own portfolio-company work (post-acquisition product selection and AI-driven development across a cluster of combined companies) could be a relevant downstream use case even if not classic due diligence.

## Diligence / Value Creation Priorities

- AI-native development capability (or the lack of it) inside a target is increasingly a signal of whether a software company is a going concern or a company "dying on the vine."
- Proprietary, non-public data flowing through the product is the clearest lever he named for improving deal terms and multiple — worth probing explicitly in any product narrative.
- Public or easily-replicated data/product capability is a risk flag under his thesis, since AI has removed the head start that used to protect it.
- Standard tech DD, in his experience, still tends to stop at "is it stable and does it work," leaving the deeper "is the product actually differentiated" question under-addressed — consistent with what we've heard elsewhere.

## Positioning And Messaging Implications

- Avoid pitching broad, generalist "product health check" diligence to PE post-acquisition audiences — Dean's direct experience is that this doesn't move decision-making.
- Lead with data: frame the diligence question as "what proprietary data does this product generate, and what is it worth," which is closer to language PE principals already use when they talk about multiple.
- For pre-acquisition/thesis-building audiences like LCV, a weak product isn't necessarily a negative finding — the framing should allow for "this is fixable and here's the upside," not just risk-flagging.
- Emphasise narrow, technically deep expertise (specific IP, specific infrastructure, specific data assets) over broad product review, since that is the one lane he explicitly said still has room.
