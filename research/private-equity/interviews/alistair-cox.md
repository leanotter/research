## Snapshot

This was a working lunch between Gareth Jones and Alistair Cox, former Chief Executive of Hays plc and now a non-executive director, where Gareth talked through his advisory business (product/tech work for scale-ups and exit-bound founders, plus the newer investor-side due diligence and post-acquisition integration offer) and asked Alistair to pressure-test how to pitch it to PE and VC. Alistair's core contribution was a blunt, first-hand operator's view of how PE/VC actually think: they are "spreadsheet shoppers," not operators, motivated almost entirely by entry price, exit multiple and IRR, and largely deaf to product-quality or vision-led language.

## Key Commercial Insights

### 1. PE/VC are "spreadsheet shoppers," not operators

Alistair's central framing: PE and VC people are populated by banker types who can run a spreadsheet and think about capital structures, but have a fundamentally superficial view of how a business actually works. The only thing that ever really matters to them is the number — what did I pay, what do I sell it for, what's the IRR/multiple in between. Product vision, ESG, and "business person" language largely wash over them, because they are not, in his framing, business people at all.

### 2. Position around returns, not product quality

Rather than pitching "I'll help you advise on whether your tech and product is okay" (which he warned will make most of them glaze over), Alistair's recommended framing was explicitly financial: "I will maximise your returns by eliminating the typical pitfalls in the product journey." The evidence base should be the percentage of deals with suboptimal returns traceable to product failure — poor integration, inadequate skills, lack of leadership, or building a product without ever validating the underlying problem.

### 3. Returns are set the day you sign the cheque

His most repeated line: "you make all your money the day you sign the cheque." Overpay, and there is no recovering it — a monkey can generate a good return if the entry price is right, and no amount of operational excellence afterwards reliably closes a gap created by paying too much. This is the foundation for positioning the audit as pre-deal protection rather than a post-deal nice-to-have.

### 4. The audit is armour for negotiation, not just insight

He compared the product/tech audit directly to a building survey: if the audit comes back clean, the buyer can pay full price with confidence; if it surfaces real issues, it becomes concrete ammunition to renegotiate price down ("I need a new roof, that's 100 grand, let's knock 100 grand off"). This reframes the commercial value of the work from "we'll tell you if it's good" to "we'll get you a better price either way."

### 5. Mid-market is the right target, not the bulge bracket

Large, brand-name PE firms and top-tier VCs believe they already have this covered — they have a "central" resource and a PwC relationship, and will politely disengage. His clear steer was toward mid-market regional PE and smaller VC firms with software/services portfolios, who typically have a generalist partner who "knows a bit" about tech but cannot genuinely run a product audit themselves. He named Sovereign and Parliament specifically as plausible targets, and flagged smaller players like LDC as lacking central capability entirely.

### 6. Portfolio-wide value creation is a largely untapped adjacent opportunity

Beyond single-deal diligence, Alistair agreed there is a real gap in PE firms sharing operating knowledge across a portfolio of 10-50 companies — most funds never systematically pool product or go-to-market learning across their holdings, despite sitting on a lot of repeatable insight. He linked this to PE's structural blind spot: because they are financially rather than operationally minded, "share ideas across the CTOs of your portfolio" simply isn't a thought that occurs to them unless someone puts it in front of them.

## How This Market Buys

- PE/VC firms run a fixed, cookie-cutter deal process focused on price, structure and exit multiple; anything outside that frame has to be translated into financial language to land.
- Large, bulge-bracket firms default to established relationships (PwC for tech, Goldman/Morgan Stanley for banking) and will assume they already have the relevant capability covered internally or via those relationships.
- Mid-market and regional PE/VC firms typically have one partner who covers software/services generally but lacks the bandwidth or specialism to run a genuine product audit themselves — this is the more realistic entry point.
- Relationships matter more than cold outreach: Alistair's own suggested next step was a personal, informal introduction and a "road test" coffee with a known contact, not a structured sales process.
- Investment committees are structurally risk-averse — their function is to kill weak deals, not evaluate vision — so a pitch has to reduce perceived risk, not just describe capability.

## Where Our Offer Fits

## Diligence / Value Creation Priorities

- Standard tech DD (as run via Golden Hill Capital-style investment bankers) focuses almost entirely on the tech stack itself — is it modern, is it in the cloud, is it "on dot net" — which Alistair and Gareth both agree is now table stakes rather than a genuine differentiator.
- The real risk sits in execution: prioritisation, product delivery, and go-to-market conversion, not the underlying technology choices.
- Direct, unfiltered customer interviews (not a CEO-curated shortlist of favourable references) are core to Gareth's method and were specifically endorsed by Alistair as the credible way to surface real signal rather than rehearsed answers.
- A structured risk register — translating customer and product findings into concrete, negotiable line items — is the deliverable format Alistair responded to most positively, since it converts soft findings into hard pricing leverage.
- Post-acquisition integration is flagged as equally important to pre-deal diligence, and as commercially valuable, since roughly 70% of tech acquisition failures were attributed (per Gareth's pitch) to poor due diligence and poor post-acquisition product integration combined.

## Positioning And Messaging Implications

- Lead with returns and risk reduction, not product quality or vision — Alistair was explicit that vision/ESG/product language "glazes them over."
- Use a concrete, evidence-backed statistic (the proportion of acquisitions or funds with suboptimal returns tied to product failure) rather than a qualitative capability pitch.
- Frame the audit as protective and self-interested for the buyer: "you make all your money the day you sign the cheque" is a strong, borrowable line for why this has to happen before the deal, not after.
- Target messaging at mid-market firms specifically; a pitch aimed at bulge-bracket PE/VC risks being politely waved off as "already covered."
- Avoid technical/architecture language altogether in the pitch — that ground is already claimed by tech DD providers and PwC-style relationships; differentiate explicitly on product, execution, and commercial outcome.

## GTM / Relationship Strategy

- Prioritise mid-market regional PE and smaller VC firms with software/services portfolios (£20-100m EV deals) over bulge-bracket names.
- Use warm, personal introductions and informal "road test" conversations (a coffee, a direct ask for feedback) rather than a cold or structured sales process — consistent with how Alistair himself offered to make the next connection.
- Alistair offered a specific, live introduction to a partner at Sovereign (deal-focused, software/services) as a genuine road-test opportunity — a concrete next step worth following up.
- Named targets flagged directly by Alistair: Parliament and Sovereign (both known to him personally, Sovereign via his own board experience at BIM); LDC and similar smaller regionals flagged as lacking central product/tech capability and therefore worth targeting, though with less personal introduction leverage.
- Gareth is to send Alistair his website link for direct feedback on positioning and messaging — a useful, low-friction way to keep testing the pitch with an experienced outside reader.

## Commercial Model Considerations

- Two structures were discussed: a flat audit fee (e.g. circa £100k) followed by a separate implementation contract if the buyer proceeds; or a risk-share model with a low or no audit fee in exchange for a clip on the deal or the implementation contract.
- A hybrid was also floated: charge little or nothing for the initial audit, but take a share ("split the ticket") if the deal completes, plus a paid implementation engagement — with implementation invoiced to the portfolio company post-close rather than to the fund itself, mirroring how Golden Hill Capital was historically paid (a flat research/shortlisting fee plus a clip on the completed deal).
- A "free unless we find something" framing was also discussed as a way to de-risk the sale for a risk-averse investment committee: charge for the audit, but only up to an agreed cap if the audit finds nothing of note, charging in full if it surfaces material findings.
- Post-integration work (led by Gareth with support from Mike and others) is positioned as a natural, higher-value follow-on engagement once the initial audit and deal have completed.

## Risks / Challenges

- Bulge-bracket PE/VC firms are likely to be a poor near-term target: they believe they already have the relevant capability covered centrally or via existing advisers (PwC, Goldman, Morgan Stanley) and are unlikely to engage seriously.
- Investment committees are structurally built to eliminate risk and kill deals, not evaluate new capability providers, so the pitch has to reduce perceived risk rather than simply describe a service.
- The proposition currently spans two quite different buyer motivations (scale-ups deploying capital vs. exit-bound founders vs. investor-side DD/value creation) — Alistair's advice implies these may need distinctly different messaging depending on audience, echoing similar segmentation advice from [[guy-ballantine]].
- Much of Alistair's steer is based on his own PE/NED experience and personal network rather than a structured market scan, so specific claims (fund names, deal sizes, what Sovereign or Parliament will or won't respond to) should be treated as a strong lead worth testing, not settled fact.

## Takeaways For Us

- A fourth independent voice, and the first from an actual former FTSE-level CEO with direct PE/NED exposure, confirms the same underlying thesis: product and execution diligence is a real, structurally underserved gap in how PE and VC currently buy.
- The sharpest actionable lesson is positioning: lead with returns and risk reduction, evidence it with a clear statistic, and frame the work as negotiating leverage for the buyer — not a product-quality assessment for its own sake.
- Mid-market PE/VC, not the largest firms, is repeatedly confirmed (here and in [[matthew-hewlett]] and [[fergus-brownlee]]) as the more realistic near-term buyer segment.
- Portfolio-wide value creation across a fund's existing holdings is a second, credible opportunity worth developing alongside single-deal diligence.
- Follow up on the concrete offer of a Sovereign introduction — this is a live, low-friction way to road-test the pitch with a real prospective buyer rather than another advisory conversation.
