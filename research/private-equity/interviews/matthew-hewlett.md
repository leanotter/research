## Snapshot

Matthew is Head of Strategy & Corporate Development at IRIS Software Group, with 12 years around IRIS and 14 years in and around the HG portfolio, so he sees both sides of a very acquisitive PE-backed software group. His core message was that product-specific diligence is a real and persistent gap: tech DD providers are staffed by "techies" and index heavily on architecture, security, and code quality, but rarely go deep on product vision, commercial positioning, or AI defensibility. That gap is more pronounced the further down the deal-size scale you go, since full-scale vendor DD (of the kind IRIS itself commissioned from EY-P on its own sale) tends to only get proactively commissioned above roughly £50-60m of revenue.

His clearest steer was on go-to-market: founders themselves rarely know they need this kind of work, so the more credible route in is through investment bankers and advisers, especially boutique, software-focused ones, who have been burned before by deals that unravelled mid-process once the product or tech story fell apart. Gareth's own experience running product at Thomas through two acquisition processes reinforced this from the buy-side/operator angle, pointing to the same underlying pattern: DD gets treated as table stakes on the tech side, but the real value loss happens where product prioritisation and go-to-market execution are never properly scrutinised before or after the deal.

## Key Commercial Insights

### 1. Full vendor-side product/tech DD is a large-deal phenomenon

On IRIS's own sale roughly two years ago, a full piece of product and tech DD work was commissioned in conjunction with EY-P, who Matthew described as the go-to specialists for very large deals — generally down to around £50-60m of revenue. Below that, and especially in the £30-40m range, IRIS sees much less proactive vendor-side tech or product work; DD at that scale tends to be led and commissioned by the buyer instead of prepared in advance by the seller.

### 2. The buy-side DD market is still maturing, and is skewed toward tech over product

For buy-side support below the very largest deals, IRIS has used firms like BearingPoint and, more extensively historically, Crosslake. Matthew's clear view is that this market "indexes a bit more on the engineering than it does on the product or commercial" — strong on architecture, code/security scans, open-source vulnerability checks, tech stack and scalability/deprecation risk, but comparatively surface-level on roadmap credibility, competitive positioning, and whether the product vision actually matches what has shipped.

### 3. DD providers are techies, and product diligence is a different skillset

Asked directly why product doesn't get the same depth of scrutiny as tech, Matthew's answer was simple: the people delivering these engagements are overwhelmingly engineers by background, and product diligence needs a genuinely different skillset. He flagged one adjacent firm, Cruxy, doing "growth intelligence" work closer to product-market fit, customer segmentation, and TAM/market modelling — more management-consultant in style — but even that isn't the kind of close-in product review we're describing.

### 4. AI and cyber risk are pushing this work earlier and making it more proactive

He expects investment committees to have permanently heightened risk sensitivity around tech defensibility and cybersecurity following the last six to twelve months, and thinks it's now more likely that a business gets this kind of work done proactively, in preparation for a process, rather than leaving a buyer to reach its own conclusions mid-deal.

### 5. Deals rarely fail because of a single product flaw — they fail through retention

Very rarely does something specific "in the product" itself kill a deal outright. Far more common is that weak retention or churn metrics surface, and those numbers are usually a downstream symptom of a product or onboarding issue (or of prior over-selling) rather than the market simply moving away. Because most of what IRIS acquires is B2B SaaS, business-critical workflow software, customers rarely leave on a whim — so a retention problem is treated as a strong signal of an underlying product or satisfaction issue.

### 6. IRIS under-indexes on usage/engagement analytics relative to attachment rates

When asked whether IRIS looks at stickiness, active-user trends, or similar behavioural analytics, Matthew's honest answer was that they look more at product/module attachment rates (since most of what IRIS acquires is cross-sold across the firm) than at granular engagement metrics, and that this is "probably a shortcoming for us... an area we should improve."

### 7. Gareth's operator lens: tech DD is table stakes, but execution is where value is actually won or lost

Having been through two acquisition processes as Head of Product at Thomas, Gareth's experience was that tech DD (which is now table stakes) is where scrutiny stops, while the real determinant of deal value — whether the business can actually turn that tech stack into shipped product and go-to-market results — goes largely unexamined. He described Thomas spending heavily on "the best stack you could get" while still struggling for years to get product out the door, and flagged post-acquisition integration as a second, related place where value consistently gets undermined. He also felt the DD provider shortlists put in front of him were generic and not well matched to the specific market.

## How This Market Buys

- Above roughly £50-60m of revenue, full vendor-side product/tech DD tends to be commissioned proactively by the seller, typically via a specialist like EY-P.
- Below that (particularly in the £30-40m range), tech and product DD is more often buyer-led and reactive rather than seller-prepared.
- For buy-side support outside the very top tier, IRIS has used BearingPoint and (historically, more heavily) Crosslake; both skew toward architecture and technical risk over product or commercial analysis.
- Founders themselves are not a reliable entry point because they typically don't know what they need — the people who do are investment bankers and advisers who have been through, or been burned by, failed or bumpy sale processes.
- Boutique, software-focused investment banks are likely to be a better first port of call than generalist ones, since they will already appreciate the tech/product risk story.

## Where Our Offer Fits

- Sell-side / exit readiness: strongest fit for founder-owned or founder-led software businesses below the scale where full EY-P-style DD gets proactively commissioned (roughly sub-£50-60m revenue), as an independent set of eyes ahead of a process.

## Diligence / Value Creation Priorities

- Standard tech DD covers: architecture review, code and security scans, open-source vulnerability checks, language/tech stack choice, deprecation and scalability risk.
- Roadmap and USP coverage exists within tech DD today but is described as "surface level" rather than a genuine product-vision or execution review.
- Team quality is assessed, but largely through an engineering-credibility lens ("do they talk the talk as an engineer") rather than a product-leadership one.
- Retention and churn are the primary lens IRIS actually uses to judge product health in a deal, more than granular usage or engagement metrics.
- Product/module attachment rates matter a lot given IRIS's cross-sell model, more so than weekly/monthly active-user trends, which Matthew flagged as an under-covered area.
- AI defensibility (is the product easily replicated, is the codebase modern enough, is there a genuine moat) is becoming one of the first questions raised in a diligence process.

## Positioning And Messaging Implications

- Frame the offer explicitly as filling the product/commercial gap left by architecture-heavy tech DD, not as a replacement for firms like EY-P, Crosslake, or BearingPoint.
- Target businesses below the revenue threshold (roughly £30-60m) where proactive, seller-commissioned product/tech DD isn't the norm today, but where the risk is just as real.
- Lead with retention, churn, and product-adoption evidence, since that is the language and the failure mode IRIS already recognises rather than an abstract "product strategy" pitch.
- AI defensibility and competitive positioning are current, heightened-risk themes for investment committees right now — a timely wedge into the conversation.
- Don't pitch founders directly as the primary buyer; they are unlikely to self-identify the need. Pitch through the advisers who prepare them instead.
